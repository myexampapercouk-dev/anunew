The problem in the file, not the tablet

Why India's quality story keeps tripping on documentation, and why market acceptance turns on fixing it.

By The Corridor Desk·Dateline Mumbai·schedule7 min read

When an FDA investigator at an Indian contract testing laboratory this year found preventive-maintenance forms already signed and dated for work not yet done, and a manager backdating analytical records to a date months in the past, the citation that followed was familiar in a way that ought to trouble the industry. The finished chemistry was not the issue. The record of it was. The warning letter to that Hyderabad laboratory, issued in March, noted that the non-contemporaneous recording of information was a repeat observation, cited across more than one inspection. It is a small case. It is also the whole pattern in miniature.

Start with the scale that makes this matter. India is one of the two largest sources of the world's medicines and a foundation of the American generic supply, and the great majority of its facilities pass inspection without a warning letter. None of what follows is an argument that Indian manufacturing is unsafe. It is an argument about a specific, stubborn and disproportionate weakness, and about the fact that the people who buy from India know exactly where it lives.

The weakness is data integrity. Across all FDA drug warning letters in fiscal 2025, integrity-related findings appeared in roughly 15 percent of cases, according to analysis of the agency's actions. For letters issued to Indian sites, the figure was closer to 60 percent. The gap is not new, and it is not narrowing quickly. Between 2022 and 2024 the FDA ran 114 surprise inspections at Indian facilities; 94 of them produced Form 483 observations. In the first quarter of this year alone, five Indian firms received warning letters that the agency framed not as sloppy paperwork but as deliberate falsification, in at least one instance involving records recreated for an investigator while the originals sat elsewhere in the building.

Two things are changing the pressure. The first is volume: the FDA issued substantially more drug warning letters in fiscal 2025 than the year before, even while carrying dozens of unfilled investigator posts, which tells you the agency is targeting harder rather than inspecting more evenly. The second is method. The agency has put an internal analytical tool, which it calls Elsa, into wide use among its staff, using it to sift adverse-event data, past 483 observations and compliance anomalies to decide which facilities to visit. Risk-based targeting rewards a clean documentation history and punishes a patchy one, and it has a long memory.

The reason this belongs at the centre of the corridor conversation, rather than in a compliance footnote, is that quality is the currency of market acceptance. An Indian manufacturer's cost advantage means nothing to a US or European buyer who cannot stand behind the data package. And the timing is pointed: with Washington due to decide within the year whether to extend tariffs to generics, the industry's argument for staying exempt rests partly on being seen as a reliable, well-documented supplier. Every falsified logbook is a line in the opposing brief. The fix is not more equipment. It is a culture in which the record matches what was done, the first time, every time, which is harder to build than a plant, and worth more.
