India spent a fortune to cut the China cord. The cord got shorter and thicker.

Record PLI investment, and a dependence that deepened to 73.7 percent. The paradox, and what actually works.

By The Corridor Desk·Dateline New Delhi·schedule6 min read

Here is the paradox the latest government figures lay bare. India's production-linked incentive schemes for pharmaceuticals have, by the state's own accounting, worked: they have drawn ₹46,744 crore of investment, exceeded their targets, and stood up domestic capacity that did not exist before. And over the same window, India's dependence on China for the chemical building blocks of its medicines did not fall. It rose, from 68.2 percent of the country's active-ingredient, key-starting-material and intermediate imports in 2020-21 to 73.7 percent by 2024-25, with the value of those Chinese imports climbing from ₹18,646 crore to ₹27,032 crore.

Both facts are true at once, and holding them together is the beginning of understanding the corridor. The bulk-drug incentive scheme, backed by a ₹6,940 crore outlay, has approved 48 projects; by June this year, thirty-nine of them, covering twenty-eight products, had been commissioned, and investment under the programme had passed ₹5,210 crore. The government points, fairly, to 191 of 726 targeted ingredients now being made in India for the first time. This is not a policy that failed. It is a policy that ran straight into the reason the dependence exists.

That reason sits at the bottom of the value chain, where it is least visible and hardest to move. Making a finished drug is a stacked process: key starting materials feed intermediates feed active ingredients feed the dose, and China's grip is tightest at the base of that stack, in the low-margin, effluent-heavy chemistry that Western and even Indian producers walked away from over two decades. India can stand up capacity for a named active ingredient and still buy the starting material for it from China. The Department of Pharmaceuticals told Parliament in March that China accounted for 70 percent or more of India's imports across a wide range of critical ingredients in both of the last two fiscal years.

Price keeps the door open. Every time Indian capacity comes online in a product, Chinese suppliers have room to cut. Discounts of 40 to 50 percent have been reported in specific molecules, and a cost-driven generic industry with thin margins responds to price. Incentives can offset the gap for a while; they cannot repeal it. The fragility of the arrangement showed again in April, when an Indian industry body asked the government to help secure basic solvent feedstocks after inventories fell during the West Asia crisis, a reminder that the exposure runs all the way down to commodity chemicals.

The honest conclusion is that self-sufficiency was the wrong target, and resilience is the right one. India is not going to make everything at home at a competitive price, and it does not need to. What the corridor needs is fewer single points of failure in the products where one disruption stops a medicine, and on that narrower, more achievable measure, the first plants are the beginning of an answer, not the end of one. The cord is still there. It is a question of how many strands, and how quickly they can be replaced when one is cut.
